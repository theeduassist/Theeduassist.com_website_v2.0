## What Is eLearning Localization?

eLearning localization is the comprehensive instructional and engineering process of adapting a digital training course to meet the linguistic, cultural, pedagogical, and technical requirements of a specific target market.

When global enterprises train international workforces or global customer cohorts, simply translating course text through automated machine translation is rarely sufficient. Empirical research by CSA Research shows that **76% of learners demonstrate significantly higher retention and compliance adherence** when training is delivered in their native language and cultural context. A properly localized course feels as though it was authored natively for the target audience.

## Translation vs. Localization

It is critical to distinguish between these two disciplines:

* **Translation** focuses strictly on converting written or spoken words from a source language into a target language accurately while preserving semantic meaning.
* **Localization** encompasses translation but re-engineers the entire learning experience. In compliance with **ISO 17100:2015** standards, localization adapts:
  * Idiomatic expressions, colloquialisms, and regional case studies.
  * Graphical imagery, color symbolism, and visual metaphors to align with local sensibilities.
  * Date conventions, numerical punctuation (e.g., European comma decimal separators), currencies, and metric/imperial units.
  * User Interface (UI) button geometry to accommodate text expansion (e.g., German and Dutch text expands 25%–35% compared to English).
  * Complex authoring tool variables, triggers, and branching logic.
  * Synchronized voiceover tracks and automated closed captions.

## What Actually Gets Localized in an Enterprise Course?

When scoping a course localization project, instructional engineers audit several discrete layers:

### 1. On-Screen Typography & Dynamic Text
Visible slide text, callouts, and bullet lists must be extracted cleanly. In modern workflows, text is extracted into standard **OASIS XLIFF 2.1** containers so translation memory systems can maintain consistency across corporate glossaries.

### 2. Interface, Player Labels & System Feedback
All course wrapper navigation elements—"Submit Assessment," "Return to Menu," "Exit Module," and "Resources"—must be translated in the player profile. For Articulate workflows, consult our dedicated [Storyline localization guide](/resources/localization/storyline-localization-guide/) and our [Rise 360 localization guide](/resources/localization/rise-360-localization-guide/).

### 3. Media, Infographics & Layered Artwork
Any graphic containing flattened embedded text must be extracted from source layered files (PSD/AI) and re-rendered. Cultural representation in photography must also be audited to reflect the target demographic.

### 4. Audio Narration & Video Dubbing
When utilizing audio narration, organizations must balance cost and immersion between human studio voice actors and neural multilingual text-to-speech (TTS). Audio pacing dictates that timing points in on-screen animations must be adjusted to align with the new cadence.

### 5. Assessments, Scenarios & Cultural Nuance
Branching scenarios must be adapted to local operational realities. For instance, a safety scenario referencing OSHA regulations in the United States must be realigned with HSE regulations in the UK or equivalent national ministries. When testing [primary and secondary technical domain skills](/blog/primary-and-secondary-technical-domain-skills/), scenario questions must remain relevant to local operational tools.

## The Engineering Workflow (ISO 17100 Compliant)

Executing global localization across hundreds of modules requires a disciplined engineering pipeline:

### Phase 1: Source Course Lock & Asset Extraction
The master English course is finalized and frozen. Engineers extract all text strings, video scripts, audio assets, and XML manifests into standardized translation packages.

### Phase 2: Terminology Management & Translation
Certified native linguists translate strings using an approved glossary and style guide to guarantee consistency across product names and technical terminology.

### Phase 3: Course Re-Engineering & Layout Desktop Publishing (DTP)
The translated XLIFF files are re-imported into the authoring tool. Localization engineers manually inspect every slide to adjust text box boundaries, re-align button placements, and resolve text expansion collisions.

### Phase 4: Multimedia Timing & Synchronization
Audio voiceover tracks are imported. Timeline cue points and slide triggers are adjusted to ensure animations match the spoken narration seamlessly.

### Phase 5: Multi-Layer Quality Assurance (QA)
Before release, courses undergo three stages of testing:
1. **Linguistic In-Context QA:** Native linguistic specialists preview the compiled course to ensure translations make sense within the interactive context.
2. **Functional & Visual QA:** Dedicated testers verify buttons, drag-and-drops, and scoring logic.
3. **LMS Staging QA:** SCORM/xAPI packages are deployed to staging environments to verify completion reporting and score passing across platforms.
